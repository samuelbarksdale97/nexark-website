# The Goodness Constraint

## 1. Capability Is Not Permission

Reality Engineering is built around movement from a present state toward a desired state.

That immediately creates a moral problem.

A person can desire something harmful. An organization can define success in a way that extracts from workers, manipulates customers, degrades the environment, or concentrates power. A technically successful system can make a bad reality more efficient.

For that reason, Reality Engineering places an explicit constraint above capability:

> **The reality being pursued, and the means used to pursue it, must remain accountable to good.**

The governing question is:

> **Can we call what we created good?**

This question cannot be answered by one metric.

“Good” is not identical to:

- profitable;
- efficient;
- engaging;
- popular;
- personalized;
- scalable;
- automated;
- innovative;
- or technically impressive.

Those qualities may support a good outcome. They may also support exploitation, addiction, deception, surveillance, or waste.

The Goodness Constraint exists so that the discipline never confuses an increase in power with an increase in wisdom.

---

## 2. What “Good” Means in the Discipline

Reality Engineering does not pretend that every person, culture, faith, or philosophical tradition will define the good in exactly the same way.

It establishes a minimum public standard that allows plural visions of success while protecting people from being treated merely as inputs to somebody else’s desired reality.

A Reality Engineering intervention should be judged through eight commitments.

### 2.1 Truth

The system should not depend on material deception, fabricated evidence, hidden uncertainty, or claims that outrun what is known.

Truth does not require revealing every private detail. It requires that omission, framing, simulation, and persuasion do not create a materially false understanding in contexts where that understanding matters.

### 2.2 Human agency

The system should expand meaningful capacity to understand, choose, act, appeal, learn, and participate.

Convenience that makes a person permanently unable to act without the system may reduce agency even while improving short-term experience.

### 2.3 Dignity

People should not be reduced to data points, labor costs, engagement targets, behavioral vulnerabilities, or obstacles to optimization.

Dignity requires attention to how a system sees and treats the person, not only what outcome it produces.

### 2.4 Consent and participation

People should have meaningful notice, choice, or involvement at a level appropriate to the stakes.

Consent is weakened when refusal is impossible, information is incomprehensible, power is severely unequal, or the system hides what is being done.

### 2.5 Responsibility and accountability

A responsible human or institution must remain answerable for consequential outcomes.

Responsibility cannot be transferred to “the algorithm,” “the model,” “the data,” or “the automation.”

### 2.6 Proportionality and restraint

The intervention should not use more data, automation, surveillance, persuasion, force, or complexity than the legitimate purpose requires.

The question is not only whether a capability can help. It is whether this degree of capability is necessary for this problem.

### 2.7 Shared benefit and non-externalization

The system should not create private success by quietly moving costs onto workers, communities, future users, public institutions, or the environment.

A benefit is incomplete when the people who create it do not participate fairly in it or when its true costs are hidden elsewhere.

### 2.8 Stewardship across time

The intervention should be evaluated not only at launch but through maintenance, scaling, dependency, and second-order effects.

A system that is acceptable at ten users may become harmful at ten million. A temporary assistant may become infrastructure. A helpful default may become an invisible form of control.

These commitments align with established responsible-AI principles that emphasize human dignity and rights, transparency, accountability, human oversight, sustainability, fairness, privacy, safety, and prevention of harm [E-007; E-008; E-009]. Reality Engineering incorporates those principles into the broader question of deliberate state transformation.

## 3. The Four Levels of Goodness

The Goodness Constraint operates at four levels.

### 3.1 The desired state

Is R★ worthy of pursuit?

A desired state may be clear, measurable, and achievable while still being unworthy. Increasing conversion through deception is not redeemed by accuracy. Reducing labor cost by creating intolerable work is not automatically good operations.

### 3.2 The intervention

Are the means used to pursue R★ acceptable?

A good goal does not authorize unlimited surveillance, manipulation, coercion, extraction, or risk.

### 3.3 The experienced reality

How do people inside the system actually experience R₁?

Design intent is not enough. People may experience confusion, exclusion, dependence, pressure, or loss of meaning that was invisible to the architects.

### 3.4 The scaled reality

What becomes normal if the intervention succeeds?

Scaling changes power, dependency, infrastructure, environmental demand, and the ability of people to opt out. A system should be judged partly by the world it rehearses.

## 4. The Goodness Review

The Goodness Review occurs twice: before contact and after contact.

## 4.1 Pre-contact review

Before deployment, answer:

### Purpose

- What legitimate human or organizational good is being pursued?
- Is the proposed intervention necessary to pursue it?
- Is there a less intrusive or harmful path?

### Truth

- What claims will the system make or imply?
- What uncertainty must remain visible?
- Could a reasonable person mistake simulation, recommendation, or generated content for verified fact?

### People

- Who is affected directly and indirectly?
- Who has power to approve?
- Who carries risk without having authority?
- Who is absent from the design process?

### Agency

- What new capability does the system give people?
- What ability, judgment, or option might it remove?
- Can people understand, contest, override, or leave the system where appropriate?

### Data and privacy

- What information is collected?
- Is each category necessary?
- Who can access it?
- How long is it retained?
- What could happen if it is exposed, combined, or repurposed?

### Labor and burden

- What work is removed?
- What work is created?
- Does burden disappear or move to someone less visible?
- Are people being augmented, deskilled, monitored, or displaced?

### Environment and infrastructure

- What compute, energy, equipment, or platform dependence does the intervention require?
- Is the expected value proportionate to those costs?

### Failure and misuse

- How could the system be wrong?
- How could it be abused?
- What happens when a user is vulnerable, malicious, confused, or outside the assumed context?

### Scale

- What changes if usage grows by 10×, 1,000×, or becomes a default?
- Does the system concentrate control or distribute usable capability?

### Responsibility

- Who is accountable for decisions, maintenance, incidents, appeals, and retirement?

## 4.2 Post-contact review

After real use, answer:

- Did people experience the benefit we intended?
- Did trust increase or decrease?
- Did anyone lose meaningful agency?
- Was consent real in practice?
- What burden moved?
- What behavior did the system reward?
- What workarounds or resistance appeared?
- Were errors understandable and correctable?
- Did the human boundary hold?
- What unintended dependencies were created?
- Did the intervention produce a reality we still believe should scale?

The post-contact review can overrule the original justification.

## 5. Disqualifying Conditions

Some findings should pause, redesign, or stop an intervention.

Examples include:

- material deception in a high-stakes context;
- non-consensual use of a person’s identity or likeness;
- inability to identify who is responsible for consequential decisions;
- systematic discrimination that cannot be acceptably mitigated;
- collection of sensitive data without a necessary and legitimate purpose;
- automation that removes a required avenue of appeal;
- hidden transfer of severe burden or risk to less powerful people;
- an environmental or financial cost grossly disproportionate to the value created;
- a dependency that leaves people unable to function when the system fails;
- or a desired state whose success depends on exploitation.

A disqualifying condition does not always mean the underlying goal is wrong. It may mean the current path is wrong.

## 6. Goodness Is More Than Safety

Safety asks whether a system causes unacceptable harm.

Goodness asks the larger question of what the system cultivates.

A system can be safe enough and still be empty, degrading, extractive, or misaligned with human flourishing. It can avoid catastrophic harm while narrowing creativity, weakening community, increasing dependency, or training people to surrender judgment.

Reality Engineering therefore asks both:

- **What must this system prevent?**
- **What kind of human and shared reality should it help make possible?**

This is the difference between merely controlling risk and practicing stewardship.

## 7. Stewardship

Stewardship is the acceptance that capability is temporarily entrusted to us and must be used with responsibility toward people, systems, resources, and futures we do not own completely.

A steward does not ask only, “What can I extract from this capability?”

A steward asks:

- What has been placed in my care?
- What does responsible use require?
- What must be protected?
- What should be cultivated?
- What must I refuse even when it is available?
- What should remain for the people who come after me?

In secular terms, stewardship is long-horizon responsibility under interdependence.

In the founder’s theological frame, stewardship is the responsibility of an image-bearer who can create but is not the ultimate source or owner of creation. See [The Echo of God](09_echo_of_god_theological_companion.md).

## 8. The Truth Standard for Generated Reality

Synthetic media and AI-generated experiences require special care because they can separate appearance from occurrence.

The standard is not that all imagination must look artificial. Fiction, visual effects, simulation, and artistic transformation have always existed.

The standard is contextual honesty.

Ask:

- Is the work presented as art, illustration, simulation, prediction, reenactment, advertisement, evidence, journalism, or documentary record?
- Does the audience have enough context to interpret it correctly?
- Is a real person’s likeness used with permission?
- Could the work materially damage someone’s reputation or decision-making by appearing factual?
- Does disclosure preserve the creative experience while preventing a false factual conclusion?
- Is provenance retained even when it is not placed prominently in the final creative?

AI-enhanced film can be a beautiful form of Reality Engineering when it expands human imagination honestly. The same capability can be used to counterfeit reality. The distinction is not the visual quality. It is purpose, context, consent, truth, and consequence.

## 9. The Human Boundary

The Human Boundary names what should remain meaningfully human within an intervention.

It may include:

- final accountability;
- moral judgment;
- relationship repair;
- care for vulnerable people;
- context-sensitive exceptions;
- consent;
- authorship;
- cultural interpretation;
- appeal;
- grief, faith, celebration, and other experiences whose value cannot be reduced to throughput;
- and decisions that affect rights or life opportunities.

The boundary is not fixed across all systems. A low-stakes scheduling task and a medical decision do not require the same human role.

The rule is proportionality: the greater the stakes, uncertainty, irreversibility, power imbalance, and scale, the stronger the human command and accountability must be.

## 10. Conflict Between Definitions of Good

Reality Engineering will encounter genuine moral conflict.

A worker may value job security while an owner values survival of the business. A customer may value personalization while a community values privacy. A creator may value artistic freedom while a subject values control of their likeness.

The Goodness Constraint does not eliminate conflict by declaring one metric supreme.

It requires a visible process:

1. Name the competing goods.
2. Identify the power held by each stakeholder.
3. Separate preferences from rights and duties.
4. Examine who bears irreversible harm.
5. Seek alternatives that reduce the conflict.
6. Use proportionality and the least harmful viable intervention.
7. Preserve appeal, revision, and accountability where resolution remains imperfect.
8. Document the trade-off honestly.

A decision can be responsible without pleasing everyone. It cannot be responsible while hiding whose loss made the decision possible.

## 11. The Goodness Scorecard

The scorecard supports judgment; it does not replace it.

Score each dimension from 0 to 3:

- **0 — unacceptable:** material violation or no credible protection;
- **1 — weak:** concern recognized but insufficiently addressed;
- **2 — responsible:** appropriate protection for current scale and stakes;
- **3 — exemplary:** the design actively strengthens this dimension.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Truth | Does the system support accurate understanding and visible uncertainty? |
| Agency | Does it expand meaningful ability to choose, act, learn, and appeal? |
| Dignity | Are people treated as persons rather than merely inputs or targets? |
| Consent | Is participation or data use meaningfully understood and voluntary where required? |
| Responsibility | Is an accountable human or institution clearly answerable? |
| Proportionality | Is the intervention no more intrusive or powerful than necessary? |
| Fairness | Are benefits, errors, access, and burdens distributed responsibly? |
| Privacy | Is data use necessary, bounded, protected, and governable? |
| Sustainability | Are material and environmental costs proportionate and considered? |
| Shared benefit | Does value extend beyond the narrow interests of the most powerful actor? |
| Resilience | Can people continue, recover, appeal, or exit when the system fails? |
| Long-term effect | Does scaling the system create a reality still worth inhabiting? |

Any score of 0 requires pause, redesign, or an explicit documented reason why the work should not proceed.

A high total score cannot cancel a fundamental rights violation.

---

## 12. The Final Test

At the end of the process, the practitioner should be able to say more than:

- It shipped.
- It saved time.
- It generated revenue.
- It looked real.
- It increased engagement.
- It automated the work.

They should be able to say:

- We told the truth about what we made.
- We understood who would carry its consequences.
- We preserved meaningful human responsibility.
- We used no more power than the problem required.
- We listened when the world corrected us.
- We left people with greater capability rather than deeper dependence.
- We accounted for costs that would have been easy to hide.
- We are prepared to change or stop the system if contact reveals harm.

Only then can the discipline return to its central question:

> **Can we call what we created good?**
